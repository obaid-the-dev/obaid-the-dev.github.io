Why Pakistanis Need Sunscreen Even in Winter

Cold air is not sun protection. In Lahore, Islamabad and Karachi, winter UV is lower — but it is still enough to cause pigmentation, dark spots and early ageing.

By Dermabloomy · 16 November 2026 · Also published on WriteUpCafe and as a ClickUp Doc

Every November in Pakistan, the same thing happens. The fan goes off, the jackets come out, and the sunscreen goes to the back of the shelf.

It feels logical. If you are not sweating, not burning, and the sun feels weak behind smog in Lahore or a cold morning in Islamabad, why would you need sun protection?

Because sunscreen in winter Pakistan is not about sunburn. It is about the slow, invisible damage that shows up months later as dark spots, uneven tone, melasma that will not fade, and fine lines that arrive early.

Winter sun is weaker. It is not gone.

Winter UV in Pakistan is lower — but still above the line that matters

Pakistan sits in a high-UV belt, and winter only brings the numbers down, not to zero.

In Lahore, the average maximum UV index in December and January is around 3 to 4. In Islamabad, it is around 4 in December, January and February. In Karachi, it stays higher — around 5 to 6 in November.

That matters because the World Health Organization recommends sun protection from a UV index of 3. Lahore and Islamabad sit on or above that line around midday in winter. Karachi stays above it all winter.

In other words, the “safe” winter months in Pakistan are still moderate-UV months. A 30-minute walk at noon in December in Lahore, without protection, is not the same as no exposure at all.

And the UV index only tells part of the story. It is weighted heavily towards UVB, the ray that causes sunburn. The ray that does most of the winter damage is different.

It is UVA, not sunburn, that does the winter damage

There are two main types of ultraviolet light to know:

UVB causes sunburn. It drops sharply in winter, when the sun sits lower in the sky.

UVA causes tanning, pigmentation and ageing. It drops far less between seasons, goes deeper into the skin, and breaks down collagen over time.

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) puts it plainly: the sun emits harmful UV rays year-round, and up to 80% of the sun’s UV rays can get through clouds.

UVA also passes through standard window glass. UVB mostly does not. That is why you can sit by a sunny window in your office in Islamabad, drive for an hour in Lahore, or work beside a window at home — and still get a daily dose of UVA without ever feeling hot or burnt.

For Pakistani skin, this matters more, not less. Brown and olive skin makes melanin quickly. Small, daily doses of UVA keep telling the skin to make more pigment. If you already have melasma, old acne marks or uneven patches, skipping sunscreen for four winter months can quietly undo much of the progress you made in summer.

That is why dermatologists talk about sunscreen as a daily habit, not a summer product.

Smog is not sunscreen

This is the most misleading winter myth in Punjab.

From October to February, smog hangs over Lahore, Faisalabad and Gujranwala. The sky turns grey-yellow, the sun looks dim, and it feels as if nothing could get through.

Smog does scatter and absorb some sunlight, so UV is often lower on a heavy smog day. But how much lower changes from day to day, and nobody can judge it by looking at the sky.

Smog also makes the problem worse in another way. Fine particulate matter, PM2.5, settles on the skin and into pores. Dermatology research links heavy pollution exposure to oxidative stress, a weaker skin barrier, and more pigmentation and dullness over time. Pollution and UV damage add to each other.

So a grey smog day is not a free pass. Treat it as “probably lower, not zero” — and if you are trying to fade dark spots, keep protecting your skin through smog season.

Cleanse gently in the evening to remove that pollution layer, moisturise well, and do not drop the morning sunscreen step.

Snow and altitude make winter UV stronger, not weaker

If your winter takes you north, the rules change again — upwards.

Fresh snow can reflect up to 80% of UV back at you. You get hit from above and from below at the same time, including under the chin, under the nose, around the ears and under the eyes — the places people usually miss.

Altitude adds more. The air is thinner higher up, so it filters out less UV. That is why a bright day in Murree, Hunza, Skardu or Naran can burn and tan you faster than a similar day in the plains, even when it feels freezing.

If you are travelling north in winter, sunscreen is not optional. Use a broad-spectrum SPF 50, reapply every two hours outdoors, and do not forget SPF lip balm, ears and the back of the neck.

Who should be extra careful in winter?

Everyone benefits from daily sunscreen, but these groups should treat it as non-negotiable in winter:

  • Anyone treating melasma, dark spots or post-acne marks. UV is one of the biggest triggers for pigmentation coming back.
  • Anyone who has recently had a facial, peel, laser or other skin procedure. New skin is more sensitive to UV.
  • People who drive a lot, or sit by a window all day. UVA comes through glass.
  • People who spend time outdoors at midday — school runs, markets, construction, field work, or a daily walk.
  • Anyone going to the northern areas for snow, hiking or sightseeing.

If you are in one of these groups, winter is not a break from sunscreen. It is when consistency matters most, because the damage is silent.

How to make winter sunscreen easy

Winter sunscreen fails for a practical reason: the summer formula feels wrong in cold, dry air.

A light gel that felt perfect in July can feel tight and drying in January. That is why many people stop — not because they decided sunscreen does not matter, but because the product stopped feeling comfortable.

Fix the formula, not the habit:

  1. Switch texture for winter. In winter, most people in Pakistan do better with a cream or lotion sunscreen, especially if skin feels dry or tight. Keep the gel or fluid for oily skin in summer, and use a more hydrating formula now. If you have oily or acne-prone skin, look for a lightweight, non-comedogenic lotion rather than a heavy cream.
  2. Use the right SPF. The AAD recommends SPF 30 or higher, every day. In Pakistan, where midday summer UV is extreme and most people apply less than the lab amount, Dermabloomy recommends broad-spectrum SPF 40 to SPF 50+ with PA+++ or PA++++ for everyday use. If you are unsure which number and finish suit your skin type, read this guide on how to choose the right SPF.
  3. Apply enough, in the right order. Most adults need about two finger-lengths of sunscreen for the face and neck. Apply it as the last step of your morning routine, after moisturiser, about 15 minutes before going out. Let it set before makeup.
  4. Do not forget the missed spots. Ears, neck, back of the hands and lips. Hands age visibly in winter, and they are almost always exposed.
  5. Reapply when you are actually outside. You do not need to reapply every two hours if you are indoors all day. You do need to reapply every two hours outdoors, and after sweating heavily — even in winter, on a hike or a long day out.

Make it boring and automatic: moisturiser, sunscreen, out the door. No decision to make each morning.

The bottom line

You do not need sunscreen in winter in Pakistan because winter sun is strong. You need it because winter sun is present — every day, through clouds, through smog, through glass, and reflected off snow in the north.

UVB may be weaker in December. UVA is not taking the winter off. And UVA is the ray most linked to the dark spots, uneven tone and early ageing that Pakistani skin is most prone to.

So keep the bottle out this winter. Change the formula if you need to, keep the SPF at 30 or higher, and let the habit do the quiet work.

Your summer skin will thank you for it.
